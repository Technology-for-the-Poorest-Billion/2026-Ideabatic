# Problem description

SMILE
The SMILE project aims to tackle the challenges associated with last-mile vaccine delivery and user error during transportation and administration. The “last mile” refers to the final stage of the supply chain, where vaccines are transported from storage warehouses to remote villages. Throughout this journey, vaccines must be kept within a strict temperature range of 2–8°C to maintain the cold chain. However, long transport times and improper handling often result in vaccine spoilage. Current cool box designs present several issues that can compromise vaccine safety. SMILE has been developed as a solution to protect temperature-sensitive vaccines during both transport and administration. It consists of a vaccine carousel surrounded by insulating walls and it surrounds an ice-pack.

Key features of the SMILE system include:
- A self-closing door to prevent the lid from being accidentally left open during vaccine administration, reducing heat exposure.
- A single ice pack design, which prevents the use of excessive or insufficient ice while remaining chemical-free.
Separation between the ice pack and the vaccine chamber, ensuring vaccines are protected from freezing.

The problem 

The majority of current vaccine coolers use a 4 ice pack design, providing uniform cooling of the vaccines. In the SMILE, a single ice pack is used to reduce human error, but there is an issue of asymmetrical cooling associated with this. Thermal tests have shown that the top vaccine chambers are hotter than the bottom chambers. Given the vaccines need to stay within such a tight temperature range, if we can investigate what is causing this asymmetry and prototype potential solutions, we can extend the cool life of the vaccines. In the original SMILE, extra insulation was added at the top leading to an asymmetric centre, however, the SMILE Go doesn’t have this.


# Presentation of the proposal

We want to investigate the causes and ways to reduce asymmetric cooling. We have a few ideas on what the possible causes are, namely the air gap at the top when the ice melts. Over the course of the next few weeks, we will:
- Design & test cooler insert
- Investigate impact of convection
- Prototype new carousel design to reduce convection
We will use temperature sensors to first test the bottle without any changes as a control test. As we propose design changes, such as the insert or the new carousel, we will perform temperature tests again to see if there are significant changes. We will also analyse the trade off between increasing insert volume and decreasing the water content. 


# Assessment of the quality of the proposal

In the wider context, this project will be:
- Responsible - The proposal should not cause any harm. We are doing this by using no chemicals in the icepack, the icepack bottle is made of HDPE which is widely recyclable. We aim to use sustainably sourced or recycled materials which don’t impact workers health
- Responsive - this means it responds to a genuine need. There are currently existing solutions to the problem e.g. a standard vaccine cooler, but this solution is an improvement. Our goals and projects aims have been developed in line with talking to our partner Kitty and looking at research she has already conducted. Our work aims to investigate one of the problems her product is experiencing.
- Adds significant value - making the vaccine cooling last longer and more even
- Accessible to end users - the modifications we are making don’t really affect use. Any insert we design should be simple and a potential redesign of the carousel should not decrease its usability
- Context appropriate - It is being developed for use in countries such as Cameroon where the ambient temperature is significantly hotter than the UK so the cooling aspect is more important. Additionally, the aim is for the product to be manufacturable in a makerspace in the country of use, so it should be 3d printable and use accessible materials.
