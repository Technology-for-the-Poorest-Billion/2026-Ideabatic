# Material research for the insert test

## Prototype material and manufacturing choice:

The inserts tested in this experiment were 3D printed from standard PLA filament. 3D Printed PLA was the simplest and most straightforward choice for the manufacture of the cooler inserts for our testing purposes, being cheap, quick to use with complex geometries, and widely available for the experiment in the Dyson Centre. It is also resistant to warping under reduced temperatures, including that below 0℃. Hot glue was used to create airtight seals at the joining locations for the PLA and the bottle. This was the most suitable option for rapid prototyping as the glue dried quickly and was able to be removed relatively easily.

## Performance limitations:

It is noted that the 3D printed PLA becomes brittle at low temperatures, which may lead to cracking or fracturing under load. In the ideal case, the cooler insert should not be under load very much, since it simply segregates the sections of the bottle. However, if too much water is poured into the cooler bottle and frozen, it may expand excessively and create stresses in the bottle and the cooler insert, leading to failure of the part. In addition, repeated freezing and thawing cycles of a 3D printed cooler insert causes cyclic loading that ultimately leads to more likely failure of the component as it is used.

Beyond the temperature characteristics, it is also noted that 3D printed filament often contains microscopic voids and gaps, especially between extrusion lines, which cause the structure to absorb moisture. As a result, no 3D prints are waterproof or airtight, though it is highly water-resistant if printed to a high enough resolution. This phenomenon was observed with our PLA print on the top of the bottle, where after some time, water permeated through the print. The initial component was printed at 90% infill using a lower-precision consumer-grade printer.

PLA itself is an organic polymer that undergoes hydrolytic degradation in water which can be accelerated by high temperatures. This polymer also has a high water uptake, which over time will cause swelling which results in potential sealing issues due to dimensional changes. The water within the PLA will experience expansion and contraction upon freezing and thawing causing mechanical failure by opening the interfaces between the layers or opening cracks. This can also increase the degradation internally and increase the porosity within the structure. For the purposes of prototyping, the uptake is low over a period of a day, so it remains suitable.

Sealing the interfaces also posed significant challenges. Although hot glue is relatively water-resistant, it is not suitable to connect the interfaces for long term use. Hot glue is weak and only weakly bonds to non-porous polymers like polyethylene, which is what the bottle is currently made of. It becomes more brittle when frozen and has been observed to peel off easily from the PLA surface. When the hot glue undergoes repeated freezing and thawing cycles, the adhesive is likely to progressively debond and lose the airtight seal, permitting water and air ingress between the inserts. One practical difficulty encountered during assembly was applying the hot glue within the bottle to join the PLA inserts to the bottle sidewalls, although careful application allowed the interfaces to be sealed sufficiently for prototype testing.

## Material and manufacture improvements:

For a low-scale, portable use, the method of 3D printing should not be changed. However, waterproof and airtight properties can be improved by using other materials. PETG is also a commonly used filament for FDM and is a naturally water-resistant material. This material exhibits lower water absorption than PLA, which is more suitable for this use case. PETG is also less brittle at low temperatures, demonstrating its suitability to this application. (Moreno Nieto et al., 2021) Unfortunately, this material is unavailable in the Dyson Centre to print with.

Absorption rates into the 3D print change depending on the percentage of infill and the presence of internal voids and surface defects. Subsequent prototypes after the initial prototype were produced using a higher quality Bambu Lab printer where the top and bottom densities were 100% and the infill density was 15%. This demonstrated improved print consistency and reduced porosity while still retaining enough flexibility to be able to be inserted into the bottle. The Bambu Lab printers also demonstrate better first layer quality, without the need for a raft. This printer also has better temperature and motion control, allowing for a more consistent finish on each layer without air gaps. Since this print is done inside an enclosed chamber, there is reduced warping during the printing process.

Coating sealant for the PLA such as epoxy can be used to make the print waterproof. Epoxy cures in anywhere from 12 to 24 hours, which for our timescales is excessive and would place pressure on our project timeline. The epoxy would flow under gravity whilst it cures in the bottle. In contrast, the higher viscosity of hot glue allows it to hold its shape relatively well before it solidifies. However, this cannot be used in replacement to the hot glue which bonds the inserts to the bottle walls. One alternative is the use of silicone sealant. Silicone remains flexible at low temperatures and is highly resistant to water ingress, making it well suited to repeated freeze-thaw cycles. The flexibility of the sealant also allows it to accommodate differential thermal expansion between the bottle and insert, reducing the likelihood of seal failure. However, silicone typically requires curing times of 24 hours or more, making it less suitable for rapid prototyping.

## Production scale solutions:

Assuming the workshops in Cameroon have access to 3D printers, these cooler insert designs can be easily created and produced. However, these inserts will not provide a sufficiently durable seal over the intended 10 year service life of the vaccine cooler. The exact lifespan of PLA depends on the conditions. Cold water and ice does not significantly degrade the PLA as much as other factors such as sunlight, chemical exposure, and humidity but will still cause unreliability in the waterproofing and sealing between the chambers. Furthermore, the adhesive interfaces between the inserts and bottle walls represent potential failure points, particularly as repeated thermal cycling may cause debonding and leakage between chambers.

For the depth that our investigation goes, hot glue is a viable option, but for long-term use, a more sophisticated manufacturing process is needed to create an air tight seal.

If this was produced at a large-scale production, eliminating separate inserts and adhesive joints would significantly improve durability and reliability over the lifetime of the product. extrusion blow moulding or stretch blow moulding the bottle with a certain amount of chambers could be used. This will create better airtight and watertight seals in comparison to the hot glue.
